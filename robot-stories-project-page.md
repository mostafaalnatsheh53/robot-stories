# Robot Stories: Project Page Texts (English, ready to paste)

## Project name
Robot Stories

## High-level summary
Robot Stories is a free, bilingual (English/Arabic) website where 17 of NASA's "abandoned" machines on the Moon, on Mars and in deep space tell school-age kids their own stories in the first person. Kids pick a world, tap a robot on a map, read its story with a real NASA photo, learn the science it made possible, and answer a quiz to earn an explorer badge. Every fact is linked to a NASA source. It turns forgotten hardware into characters kids remember, and brings this content to Arabic-speaking students who rarely get it in their own language.

## Detailed description
**What it does.** Since the 1960s NASA has left landers, rovers, mirrors, seismometers and spacecraft across the solar system. Robot Stories introduces kids to 17 of them, taken from the hardware list in the official challenge resources: the Apollo 11 lunar module "Eagle", the Apollo 11 laser reflector, the Lunar Roving Vehicles, Surveyor 3 and the Apollo seismometers on the Moon; Viking 1, Sojourner, Spirit, Opportunity, Phoenix, InSight and Ingenuity on Mars; and the Kepler Space Telescope, NEAR Shoemaker (resting on asteroid Eros), Dawn (orbiting dwarf planet Ceres), Voyager 1 and Pioneer 10 in deep space.

**How it works.**
1. Pick a world: the Moon, Mars or Deep Space.
2. Tap a glowing robot on a simple map, or pick it from a timeline ordered by year.
3. The robot introduces itself in the first person (for example: "I was supposed to work for 90 days. I kept going for almost 15 years!").
4. A "What we learned" box explains the science it made possible (the Moon drifting 3.8 cm a year, 1,319 marsquakes, water ice under Mars' soil, more than 2,600 planets found around other stars).
5. Each robot shows its status (resting forever, still useful today, still traveling), a credited NASA photo where available, and a link to its official NASA page.
6. A quick quiz earns a badge; collecting all 17 makes you a "Master Space Explorer".

**Why it matters.** Kids usually meet space history as dates and names. Telling it through the machines' own voices makes it personal and memorable, while every fact stays tied to NASA sources. A one-click Arabic version (with right-to-left layout) opens the content to Arabic-speaking children, who have very little space content like this in their own language.

**How we built it.** A single responsive web page in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no frameworks, so it loads fast on phones and school computers and needs no sign-in. Progress (badges) is saved in the browser.

**What's next.** Voice narration for younger kids, more robots (Curiosity, Perseverance, Apollo lunar modules), and printable teacher worksheets for schools in Palestine.

We used Claude (Anthropic), an AI assistant, to help write and debug the website code, draft the robots' first-person stories, translate them into Arabic, and draft the presentation and these texts. The team chose the robots and the story format, checked every fact against the NASA sources listed above, and reviewed all text. All images are real NASA photos; no AI-generated images are used.
